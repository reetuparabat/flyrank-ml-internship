Does a Trained Model Beat a Transparent Rule at Ranking Content for Refresh?

Reetu Parabat · Machine Learning Track Capstone · github.com/reetuparabat/flyrank-ml-internship
Abstract

Which pages in a client's content library are worth reviewing first for refresh, expansion, protection, pruning, or monitoring — the exact tension behind FlyRank's Lane 2 problem, where editorial hours are always scarcer than the content library they have to cover — and can a simple trained model beat a transparent, hand-written rule at ranking them? Built on the FlyRank ML Internship's 30,000-row content-performance dataset spanning 32 pseudonymous clients, drawn from a 79-million-row production search-data warehouse. A Logistic Regression classifier, validated with a client-grouped train/test split and an empirical leakage-injection audit, is compared against a transparent CTR-underperformance baseline on identical held-out data. The baseline's ranking ability was statistically indistinguishable from random (AUC 0.508), while the model reached 85% precision in its top 20 ranked pages against a 51.7% base rate Observed — and a naive random split alone would have overstated the model's AUC by 0.113 Verified. The output is a ranked, human-reviewed content-action playbook, not an automated publishing system: every recommendation carries a plain-language reason and a confidence band tied to its validated precision.

01Introduction

This is where the research question comes from, in practice — not a hypothetical, the actual operating problem behind FlyRank's Lane 2: content refresh opportunity scoring.

Picture a content team managing a client's site: a few thousand pages, built up over years, covering products, guides, and category content. Search rankings and AI-answer citations shift constantly, but editorial time doesn't scale with the size of the library — a strategist might get through 20–50 pages in a focused review session, out of a portfolio that can run into the thousands. Every page is a candidate for one of five actions — refresh, expand, protect, prune, or simply monitor — and picking wrong has a real cost either way: reviewing a healthy page wastes scarce time, and missing a declining one lets it slide further before anyone notices.

The obvious first move is a hand-written rule — flag pages with poor click-through rate for their search position. That rule is transparent and easy to trust, but it optimizes for one specific symptom (CTR underperformance), not the broader question of which pages are actually declining. This paper asks whether a small, interpretable model, trained on the same data and evaluated on the same held-out split, can do meaningfully better at that broader ranking question — and whether the answer changes once the model is validated honestly rather than convincingly. (In public-safe terms throughout: all figures below come from the anonymized internship release — no client is named or identifiable, and every number is reported at the portfolio level, never tied to one account.)

Excluded, and why
  • Label-derived columns — trend_direction, trend_pct, and the six last_30d/prev_30d columns they're computed from. This wasn't assumed: the correlation between the last30/prev30 computation and the published trend_pct column was measured directly at r = 0.9999999984 Verified — these columns don't correlate with the label, they define it.
  • Identifiers — content_id / client_id are used only for grouping and validation splits, never as model features.

03Methodology

Label. is_declining_label = 1 if trend_direction == "down" — a same-window proxy (30-day vs. prior-30-day impression comparison), not a genuine future outcome. This limitation is carried through to Section 6, not hidden.

Baseline. A transparent rule: flag a page if it is visible (impressions_90d ≥ 500) and its CTR sits below the mean CTR for its own position tier. One score, one reason code, no model — the floor this work has to beat.

Model. Logistic Regression, chosen over Random Forest because it won on its own merits at every K tested on this feature set, not by default Observed — a case where the simpler model earned the win rather than assuming it. Missingness in six columns (word count, char count, search volume, competition, CPC, main intent) is systematic, tied to content_type, so each gets a _was_missing indicator flag rather than silent median imputation.

Validation design. GroupShuffleSplit by client_id (75/25) — confirmed 0 client overlap between train and test, versus 31 of 32 clients overlapping under a naive random row split on the identical data (see Results). Grouping by client, not row, is the difference between a validated result and an inflated one here.

Leakage check, performed not just claimed. impressions_last_30d was deliberately added back into the honest, grouped-split feature set. AUC jumped from 0.582 to 0.723 (+0.141) Verified — confirming both that its exclusion was correct and that this test harness actually detects leakage when it's present, rather than passing by accident.

The baseline's AUC (0.508) is statistically indistinguishable from random — not a failure of the rule so much as a mismatch of targets: it was built to rank CTR-fix opportunity, a different question than "is this page declining." The model's strongest result is at the top of the queue, precision@20 = 0.850 against a 0.517 base rate — exactly where a strategist with limited time would look first.

05Limitations & honest framing

  • Correlational, not causal. Every finding here is "these pages look worth reviewing first," never "refreshing will fix this" — no controlled experiment sits behind any number in this paper.
  • Single split, not k-fold. One grouped train/test split on 32 clients. The exact precision@K decimals could move on a different seed; the direction (model beats baseline; baseline is near-random) is the trustworthy part Directional.
  • Same-window label. The decline label compares two halves of an already-elapsed window, not a genuine future outcome. A production version needs a forward-looking label.
  • Thin-traffic blind spot. The model has close to no signal on pages under ~50 impressions/90 days — a low score there is not a validated "safe," it's an absence of evidence.
  • Scope. This model's validated range is these 32 clients and this feature set. A new client needs to go through the same grouped-split check before being trusted.

The counter-intuitive finding

71 of the top 100 highest-risk pages were updated within the last 30 days and are declining anyway — the opposite of "stale content decays." For this portfolio, the top-priority action is escalate for deeper review, not a routine refresh Directional: something other than staleness — intent mismatch, competitive pressure, or content over-production — is the more likely driver for the highest-priority group.

Must never be automated
  • No auto-publish, auto-delete, or auto-redirect triggered by this queue.
  • No client-vs-client comparison — this ranks pages within a client's portfolio, not accounts against each other.
  • No unsupervised pruning — irreversible, and exactly the archetype the model has the least signal on.
